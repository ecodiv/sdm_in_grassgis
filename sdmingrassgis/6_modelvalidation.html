6  Model evaluation

So far, we have discussed how to build a model and use it to predict the distribution of a species. However, understanding how to interpret and evaluate the results of the model is equally important. A critical step in this process, known as model validation, is assessing the accuracy of the model’s predictions.

In Section 4.3.3, we introduced the AUC-ROC, a statistic that provides a measure of model performance. However, evaluating a model on the same data it was trained on often leads to overly optimistic assessments, as models tend to perform worse on new, unseen data. To truly assess a model’s predictive power under new conditions or in untested areas, it must be tested on a separate dataset that wasn’t used during training. This is the subject of paragraph 6.1.

Another important consideration is the context in which predictions are made. For example, predictions under climate conditions that deviate strongly from those represented in the training data (novel climates) introduce uncertainty. In such cases, the model’s assumptions and extrapolations may no longer hold, potentially compromising the reliability of its predictions. This is the subject of paragraph 6.2].

6.1 Cross-validation

A common way to assess the ability of a model to generalize beyond the data on which it was trained is to split presence data into two parts: one for training the model and another for testing its performance. This approach is available in r.maxent.train through the randomtestpoints parameter. For example, we can instruct r.maxent.train to use 80% of the presence points to train the model, and the remaining 20% to test the model’s performance.

While this approach is straightforward, the drawback is that the results can vary depending on which 20% of the points are selected for testing. A more robust and commonly used technique for estimating the accuracy of a predictive model is cross-validation. There are different types of cross-validation, but we’ll focus on k-fold cross-validation here. In k-fold cross-validation, the original sample is randomly partitioned into k subsamples with an approximately equal number of records, as further explained in Figure 6.1.

Using k-fold cross-validation will give you a more reliable estimate of model accuracy, as it reduces the variability associated with a single random data split and leverages the entire dataset for both training and validation.

6.1.1 Model training

For this tutorial, we carry out a 4-fold cross-validation. To perform the cross-validation, we run r.maxent.train with the parameter replicatetype = crossvalidate and the parameter replicates = 4. This instructs Maxent to perform a 4-fold cross-validation. As a result, Maxent trains four sub-models in the background, computes validation statistics for each sub-model, and then derives average statistics across the four sub-models.

In cross-validation, multiple models are trained on slightly different subsets of the data. Technically, these are separate models because each one is fitted to a different data split and may therefore produce slightly different predictions. This variation reflects the inherent variability in the data.

Conceptually, however, these models are not independent in the same way as models trained with different algorithms. Instead, they represent repeated iterations of the same underlying model, used to assess robustness and generalizability. For simplicity, we therefore refer to these as “sub-models”.

Combining the cross-validation option with the -y will produce two new point layers: a sample prediction point layer and a background prediction point layer. In contrast to a single run (like in Step 4.2), both attribute tables are extended with two additional columns: the average predicted probability and the range of predicted probabilities across the four sub-models1.

To keep the outputs clearly identifiable, we use the samplepredictions and backgroundprediction parameters to explicitly name the point layers containing predicted suitability scores. The sample prediction points are saved as model02_suitability_samplepoints, and the background prediction points as model02_suitability_backgroundpoints2.

We use the projectionlayers parameter to specify the folder containing the environmental raster layers previously exported with r.maxent.swd. This will result in the creation of several raster prediction layers. These rasters summarize predictions across the four sub-models and represent the average, minimum, maximum, median, and standard deviation of predicted suitability. Using the predictionlayer parameter, we define model02_suitability as the root name for these outputs. The resulting raster layers share this base name and are distinguished by the suffixes _av, _min, _max, _median, and _stddev.

6.1.2 Evaluation statistics

To examine how well the four sub-models and the model overall performed, we take a look at the model statistics. We can find these statistics in the HTML file Erebia_alberganus_obs.html located in the model_02 output folder. The page provides a summary of the results from the 4-fold cross-validation and includes links to the results of the individual sub-models in the top right corner. The average test AUC across the replicate runs is 0.9250, with a standard deviation of 0.002.

  • AUC-ROC
  • Omission graph

Figure 6.2 shows the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, averaged over the four sub-models (red line). The standard deviation is represented by the blue band around the red line.

Figure 6.3 shows the test omission rate (green line) and predicted area (red line) as a function of the cumulative threshold, averaged over the four sub-models. The standard deviation of the omission rate and predicted area are illustrated by respectively the yellow and blue bands.

The validation diagnostics from each individual sub-model help indicate how the model will perform when estimating presence in unknown locations. If the model performs well for some groups, but poorly for others, we should be careful when interpreting the model outcomes. In this case, differences are fairly small (resulting in a small standard deviation).

Q 6.1 You would expect the training AUC to be higher than the test AUC. In some cases, however, the test AUC can higher than training AUC. Can you give a possible explanation?

A possible explanation is that the test data represents a smaller set of presence points, which results in a much smaller prevalence (the ratio of presence points to background points) in the test set compared to the training dataset.

Background points, especially those located in environmentally distinct areas far from known occurrences of the species, are typically easier for the model to classify as absences. This results in a higher true negative rate (specificity) in the test set.

Since the AUC (area under the ROC curve) measures both sensitivity (true positive rate) and specificity (true negative rate), an improvement in specificity can lead to an overall increase in AUC.

6.1.4 Response curves

The response curves in the HTML file occurrences.html in the model_02 folder show the mean response of the 4 sub-models (red) and and the mean +/- one standard deviation (blue, two shades for categorical variables).

  • Marginal response curves
  • single-variable response curves

The marginal response curves reveal substantial differences in stability across predictors. For some variables, the mean response curve is accompanied by a wide standard deviation across cross-validated sub-models (visible as a broad blue band), indicating high variability in the estimated effect. This variability reflects greater uncertainty in the influence of these predictors and, consequently, increased uncertainty in model predictions for locations where predictor values fall within ranges showing large dispersion among sub-models.

For certain predictors, the direction of the marginal response curve differs from that of the corresponding single-variable response curve (e.g., bio14). Such discrepancies arise when predictors interact within the multivariate model. Marginal response curves reflect the combined effects of a focal variable while accounting for the presence of other predictors, whereas single-variable response curves are fitted in isolation and ignore interactions. As a result, interactions among predictors can lead to marginal responses that differ in shape or even direction from those suggested by single-variable responses.

that several single-variable response curves exhibit pronounced oscillations, characterized by multiple peaks and troughs. These patterns are unlikely to represent biologically meaningful responses and instead suggest that the model is fitting noise rather than signal. Such behavior is commonly associated with overfitting, which may result from excessive model complexity, limited sample size, or strong collinearity among predictors. In these cases, response curves should be interpreted with caution, as they may not reflect robust or generalizable ecological relationships.

6.1.5 Using the model

We used cross-validation to evaluate the predictive power of the model (as defined by the selected parameter settings) under new conditions or in untested areas. This process produced four model variants, each trained on slightly different subsets of the data due to the cross-validation procedure. These models are described by the lambdas files in the output folder. For each of these model variants, MaxEnt generated a raster layer representing the predicted probability of occurrence. These layers are then summarized by calculating their average, median, minimum, maximum, and standard deviation. These resulting summary layers are the ones currently available in our mapset.

But what if we want to use the selected parameter settings to predict species distributions under different environmental scenarios and compare the result predicted potential distribution with the current potential distribution (as we have done in Chapter 5)? One option is to select one of the four models or compute the average probability values across all four, as done during training. However, the standard approach is to rebuild the model using all available occurrence data, ensuring that the model benefits from the full dataset. This model can then be used to make predictions under different environmental conditions or in new geographic areas.

6.2 Novel climates

Predictions from a species distribution model can be less reliable when applied to novel conditions—situations outside the range of conditions observed during model training. This happens because the model relies on relationships it learned from the training data, which may not hold true in different areas or future scenarios.

6.2.1 MES statistics

The Multivariate Environmental Similarity (MES), as described by Elith et al. [1], can be used to evaluate if and where future conditions are outside the range of conditions observed during model training, and thus where model predictions might be less reliable. In GRASS, we can use the r.mess addon to calculate the MESS.

We use the r.mess module to examine how different the climatic conditions in 2081 are to present conditions. For the present climate conditions, we use the same data and bioclimatic variables as we used to create our first model in paragraph 4.2. We only consider the conditions in the presence and background locations, as this is what we used to train the model. To do so, we combine and convert presence and background points into a new raster layer referencelayer, and use this as input for the ref_rast parameter. We create the MESS layers in the Erebia_alberganus mapset.

Step 6.5  

# Go to mapset 'model_01' and set the region
# It should already be the correct region, but
# just to be sure
g.mapset mapset=Erebia_alberganus
g.region raster=bio_1@climate

# Combine the occurrence and background points
v.patch input=model01_suitability_samplepoints,model01_suitability_backgroundpoints output=referencepoints

# Convert the point layer to a raster layer
v.to.rast input=referencepoints output=referencepoints use=value
# Go to mapset 'model_01' and set the region
gs.run_command("g.mapset", mapset="model_01")
gs.run_command("g.region", raster="bio_1@climate")

    "v.patch",

We use the ref_rast parameter to set the reference raster layer, which we create first, as the reference layer.

Alternatively, we could have used the ref_vect parameter with the referencepoints vector layer directly. If you do so, be aware that the vector layer contains multiple point within some raster cells. The r.mess module will ignore all but one with a warning message.

By first converting the point layer to a raster, and using that as input, we eliminate duplicate points up front, and thereby avoid the warning message.

To assess if and where future conditions are outside the range of conditions observed during model training, we use the same future climate data that we used as input to our model prediction in paragraph 5.2. These are the projected bioclimatic data for the period 2081-2100, based on the SSP585 climate scenario from the EC-Earth3-Veg general circulation model.

6.2.2 Results

The main MESS map (Figure 6.11) shows the degree of environmental similarity between current conditions and projected conditions in 2081 at each location. Negative values (indicated in red on the map) means there are one or more environmental variables outside the range present in the training data, so predictions in those areas should be treated with strong caution.

  • MESS
  • MOB
  • Count Negative

You can compare the MESS map with the occurrence point map (Figure 4.4), the predicted probability map under future climate conditions (Figure 5.2) and the presence-absence map (Figure 5.10) to assess the reliability of the predictions. If you zoom in, you’ll notice that for most locations where the species has been observed, conditions in 2081 are projected to remain within the range of observed values under current conditions.

We need to be more cautious about locations where the species is currently absent, but where conditions will become suitable according to model predictions. Some of these locations have a negative MESS. In other words, predictions for these locations are based on environmental conditions that fall outside the range of values observed in our current dataset. Therefore, for these locations, projected increases in habitat suitability should be interpreted with caution, as the model is extrapolating beyond its training data.
